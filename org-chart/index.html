The reasoning

Why we made each change

One principle drove most of it: if a role is called a P&L owner, it has to control the things that make that P&L, meaning what gets sold, at what price, and with whose labor. The old chart broke that rule in several places at once.

1. The CEO goes back on top

Change: CMO removed from above the CEO.

The CEO answers to the owner or board for the whole company. A CMO runs one function inside it. With the CMO on top, the person spending the marketing budget supervises the person accountable for whether that spend pays off. If that box is really the founder, the fix is to label it Owner / Board. They still get authority over the CEO, but it comes from the operating agreement, not from a marketing title. If they also run marketing day to day, draw that as a second box under the CEO.

2. Three revenue layers become one

Change: CMO, Director of Marketing, CRO and VP Sales collapse into one VP of Marketing. Selling moves to the GMs.

Marketing, booking, selling and membership are one funnel. The old chart split it across three branches, so no one owned the outcome. At $100M a CMO and a CRO is title inflation. One full-spectrum VP of Marketing covering acquisition, brand and lifecycle is enough, and this change often lowers executive cost.

3. The GMs get a P&L they actually control

Change: Comfort advisors move under an HVAC Sales Manager reporting to the HVAC GM.

This was the chart's deepest flaw. The GMs were labeled P&L owners while the CRO controlled their close rate, discounts and pricing. That setup ends in "sales sold it too cheap" versus "ops installed it wrong." With selling inside the trade, the tech-to-advisor handoff stays in one chain of command. GMs are paid on controllable contribution, not on net after overhead allocations they can't influence.

4. Booking and dispatch merge

Change: Contact center (from the CRO) and dispatch (from Field Support) go under one Director of Customer Operations.

Booking calls you have no capacity to run, or dispatching without knowing what was promised, is one of the most common failures in residential service. One owner fixes that. The risk is that Operations quietly turns down paid leads, so the merge only happens together with the anti-throttling controls listed below.

5. A field supervisor layer gets added

Change: Field supervisors at roughly 1 per 8–12 techs, carrying only a partial sales quota.

The council agreed this is the highest-return gap. One service manager can't ride along with, coach and check the callbacks of 50+ techs, so average ticket, callback rates and retention drift. Supervisors who carry a full quota stop coaching, which is why the quota is capped.

6. Install and permit coordination are treated as core work

Change: Install coordinators, a permit/inspection coordinator, and a release-to-install gate.

Install is roughly half the revenue. Without coordination, sold jobs stall with missing equipment, failed inspections or unresolved financing. No job goes on the install calendar until scope, equipment, permit path and financing are verified.

7. Overloaded boxes get split

Change: "Fleet & Warehouse" becomes purchasing, warehouse and fleet. "Safety & Training" becomes EHS, Technical Training and Quality.

Purchasing at this size covers $20–30M of equipment plus rebate programs worth real margin points, so it can't be a side job. Quality and safety must not report to the people whose numbers they grade, so QA sits outside both GMs and Safety can stop work and escalate straight to the CEO.

8. Missing owners get named

Change: Business Systems, risk and licensing, financing and rebate controls, deferred revenue, a membership program owner, and after-hours coverage.

A 500-person company running on ServiceTitan had no system owner. Licensing, which needs a named primary and backup license holder per trade and jurisdiction, had no owner either. Membership is both a capacity commitment and a deferred-revenue liability, so it needs one owner who can stop selling it in zones that can't deliver the visits.

What makes the boxes work

Controls that ship with the chart

  • Anti-throttling: GMs sign a rolling capacity plan. Capacity is published daily before the phones open. Every unbooked call gets a reason code. Paid ads pause the same day a trade or zone can't meet its service promise. Review weekly how much marketing spend went to calls that were turned away for lack of capacity.
  • Pricebook: Ops sets the labor assumptions, supply chain sets material costs, GMs set prices above the floor, and the CFO sets and enforces the floor. Business Systems publishes changes under version control.
  • Discounts and financing: approval tiers are enforced in the software (advisor, then sales manager, then GM, then CFO below the floor). The CFO caps dealer fees.
  • Incentives: each role is paid on what it controls. Don't bonus on injury counts, callback classifications or booking rate, because that corrupts the reporting.
Order of operations

What to do first

  1. Exposure audit (2–4 weeks). Check excavation and 811 compliance, driver records and DOT status, license holders and their backups, payroll and overtime accuracy, financing disclosures, and refrigerant certifications. Fix active risks immediately.
  2. Fix the top: pick Model A or B and collapse the CMO / CRO / VP Sales stack.
  3. Add field supervisors plus install and permit coordinators.
  4. Name a systems admin and a BI analyst, and produce the weekly margin and capacity view.
  5. Merge booking and dispatch, together with the controls above. This is mostly a reorg, not new hiring.
  6. Split purchasing from warehouse and fleet, and stand up independent QA and warranty recovery.
  7. Deepen Finance: deferred revenue, financing and rebate reconciliation, pricebook floors.
  8. Build technical training and the recruiting team. Add outbound and other revenue roles last.

Sanity check only: the council estimated roughly 420–550 employees at about $170K–$230K revenue per employee. If you're well below that range, the problem is productivity, not missing executives.

Still contested

Where the council didn't fully agree

  • Model A vs. B. A minority view prefers a central sales leader, because GMs promoted from the field rarely coach selling well. The majority holds Model B, on the condition that a real sales manager sits under each GM.
  • Where booking sits. A minority view would keep the call center under revenue to prevent ops from sandbagging. The majority's answer is that splitting booking from dispatch only hides the throttle in the capacity settings, so the controls matter more than the reporting line.
  • Technology's boss. CEO/COO or CFO. Resolved as: it depends on how stable your systems are.
  • Ratios. 1:8–12 supervisor spans are a starting range, not a rule. Geography and tech experience change them.
